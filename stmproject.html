Building a Desktop Scanning Tunneling Microscope

A low-cost STM built around a $1 piezo buzzer disk, an etched tungsten tip, and a spring-suspended scan head, with custom analog electronics and Teensy firmware, that can resolve individual carbon atoms on graphite!

Scanner
Piezo buzzer disk cut into four quadrants, ±10 V drive
Tip
Electrochemically etched tungsten
Setpoint
1 nA tunneling current at 100 mV bias
Current amplifier
100 MΩ transimpedance, ADA4530-1
Isolation
Four-spring suspension (~1 Hz) with eddy current damping
Control
Teensy 3.6, 25 kHz PI loop, 16-bit DAC and ADC

1Overview

The general idea is to build an STM (scanning tunneling microscope) that can see, at a minimum, individual carbon atoms in a graphite lattice. A few people have done this and posted their machine images and general ideas/process online, but not many. I'd like to continue where they left off and document this build well enough that other people can follow in my footsteps easily. The machine uses pretty cheap materials (a piezoelectric disk instead of a piezoelectric tube, tungsten wire instead of platinum-iridium wire, a manual coarse approach instead of a motorized one, etc.), but should still be functional, for a lot less money (on the order of $200-$300). The goal is to replicate their work, document the process in detail, and achieve atomic resolution on HOPG (highly oriented pyrolytic graphite), such as in the picture below. This build is heavily influenced by Dan Berard's and John Alexander's designs and their use of the 'unimorph disk scanner' concept.

2Theory of STM Operation

The in-depth theory of the STM is quite complex, so I will only provide a relatively surface-level overview here. I've put some of the more in-depth papers on the theory of scanning tunneling microscopy in the "Additional Links and Resources" section at the end of this page.

In general, a piezoelectric disk (or tube) is used to lower an atomically sharp tungsten tip to within 5-10 ångströms (0.5-1.0 nm) of the surface of the sample. When a small bias voltage is applied between the sample and the tip, electrons quantum tunnel across the gap. A tunneling current amplifier reads this current and sends it to the control electronics. The tip scans over the surface of the sample while a PI feedback loop adjusts its height to keep the current constant, and the data is sent over USB to the computer to build the image. I will cover this all in more depth below.

The parts of an STM: a piezo scanner moving a sharp tip, a current amplifier, and feedback electronics that hold the tunneling current constant while the tip scans.

2.1Tunneling Theory

Scanning tunneling microscopy (STM) tunneling theory is quite complex, but it can be simplified greatly with a few approximations. STM involves the tunneling of electrons through vacuum (or in this case, air) between the tip and the sample. To simplify, this can be treated as tunneling between two metal electrodes separated by a vacuum gap. The vacuum gap acts as a potential barrier to the electrons. In this simplified form, one can apply the textbook solution of the Schrödinger equation for a rectangular barrier:

In classical physics, if an electron has energy E smaller than the height of the barrier, there is no way for it to cross. It would first need to acquire that energy somewhere. This is like coasting down a 10 meter hill on my bike: I can make it partway up a 20 meter hill using that energy, but not all the way. I would need to get more energy somehow (by pedaling, or by starting on a higher hill) to make it over. In quantum mechanics, though, an electron with energy E below the barrier height still has a wavefunction that extends into the barrier. Instead of dropping to zero, it decays exponentially inside the barrier. If the barrier is thin enough (a few ångströms), there is a real probability of finding the electron on the other side. This is the equivalent of the biker disappearing from one side of the hill and reappearing on the other. This "quantum tunneling" through barriers that electrons shouldn't classically be able to cross is the basis for all of scanning tunneling microscopy.

The bias voltage doesn't give the electrons enough energy to get over the barrier: it's around 0.1 V, against a barrier of roughly 4-5 eV. What it does is offset the Fermi levels of the tip and sample, so more electrons tunnel one way than the other and a net current flows. With no bias, equal numbers of electrons tunnel in each direction and the net current is zero. Now let's look at the math to see how the tunneling current depends on the bias voltage, the separation distance, and the barrier height between the tip and the sample.

For a rectangular barrier, we get a wavefunction of:

Ψ(d)=Ψ(0)e−κdwhereκ=2m(Φ−E)ℏ

The probability of finding an electron behind the barrier of width d is:

W(d)=|Ψ(d)|2=|Ψ(0)|2e−2κd

In STM, a small bias voltage is applied between the sample and the tip, which sets the direction of the net tunneling current. The barrier height can be approximated by the average work function of the sample and tip materials. The work function of a material is the energy required to remove an electron from the surface of that material into vacuum. It differs between the surface and bulk of a material, but we only care about the surface here. Work functions have been measured experimentally and can be found online.

The critical parameter in this solution is κ. One finds that:

κ=2m(Φ−E)ℏ

where Φ represents the barrier potential, E is the energy of the particular state, and ħ is the reduced Planck constant. To simplify the model, Φ can be replaced by its average value across the barrier, although in general Φ would not be constant. With this simplification, the system becomes a rectangular potential barrier, which is quite easy to solve. When the bias voltage is much smaller than the work function, the inverse decay length of the electrons simplifies further to:

κ≈2mΦℏ

The current is then proportional to the probability of electrons tunneling through the barrier. From quantum mechanics, that probability is proportional to the wavefunction squared, so the current becomes proportional to:

I∝∑En=EF−eVEF|Ψn(0)|2e−2κd

Finally, by using the definition of the local density of states:

ρs(z,E)≡1ε∑En=E−εE|Ψn(z)|2

the current can be shown as:

I∝Vρs(0,EF)e−2κd≈Vρs(0,EF)e−1.025Φdwith d in Å and Φ in eV

where d is the separation between the tip and the sample. This result is the key to STM operation. If an atomically sharp tip is used, the tunneling current from the frontmost atom of the tip will be exponentially larger than from the atoms slightly behind it. Plugging typical work function values in for (Φ − E) in the equation for κ, one finds that the tunneling current decreases by about an order of magnitude for every ångström of separation between the tip and the sample.

This gives STM extraordinary vertical sensitivity. Because the current changes by about a factor of 10 per ångström, a feedback loop that holds the current constant to within a few percent holds the tip height constant to about 0.01 Å in principle. That is the sensitivity of the measurement, not the real performance of the instrument: in practice, vibration and electronic noise set the vertical noise floor, which is well above that.

Finally, for a more accurate calculation of the current density, we would need to solve Schrödinger's equation for all three regions (before, in, and after the barrier) with a vertically varying electric field, leading to a transition probability of:

T(E)≈exp(−2ℏ∫0d2m(V(z)−E)dz)

2.2Piezoelectric Scanner Theory

A piezoceramic (such as PZT) is a ceramic in which many molecular dipoles, or positive-negative charge separations, have been aligned ("poled") at an elevated temperature by applying a strong voltage across its electrodes. The ceramic is then cooled, which locks in the polarization. When a smaller voltage is later applied with the same polarity as the poling voltage, the ceramic expands in the poling direction and contracts in the perpendicular directions. In a tube, the poling is radial (through the wall), so the wall gets thicker and the tube gets shorter. In a disk, the poling is through the thickness, so the disk gets thicker and shrinks in diameter. These small strains are what move the tip.

Crystallographic conventions are used to describe the orientation of a poled piezoceramic and to characterize the performance of the various piezoceramic materials. The specific parameter we care about in an STM is:

da,b

where:

d = the piezoelectric strain coefficient, the ratio of mechanical strain developed to electric field applied. Its units are m/V, usually quoted in pm/V (10−12 m/V). For reference, 1 Å/V = 100 pm/V.

a = electrode orientation

b = direction of induced strain

For a, the only value for our application is:

3 = the direction from the positive to the negative electrode. In a tube this is through the wall thickness, since the electrodes coat the inside and outside of the tube. In a disk it is through the thickness of the ceramic.

For b, the only value for our application is:

1 = the direction of induced strain (movement) perpendicular to the field: along the length of a tube, or across the diameter of a disk.

d3,1 is the "piezoelectric coefficient" which, when you plug it into the formulas below, tells you how much the ceramic will shrink (hence the negative sign) when a positive voltage is applied. d3,1 ≈ −171 pm/V (−1.71 Å/V) for PZT-5A and ≈ −265 pm/V (−2.65 Å/V) for PZT-5H, at room temperature.

This parameter varies with temperature: the higher the temperature, the more expansion or contraction per volt. It doesn't vary much for PZT-5A (about 11% from 0-100 °C), but varies by over 30% for PZT-5H. All of the formulas below should be treated as approximations, good to roughly ±20%.

Piezo tube scanner

The piezo tube is the scanner used in most commercial STMs. The inner electrode of the tube is grounded, and the outer electrode is sectioned into four quadrants (+X, −X, +Y, −Y).

Above: Piezo tube scanner, with the inner electrode grounded and the outer electrode split into four quadrants.

When a positive voltage is applied to one of the outer quadrants, that side of the tube contracts lengthwise, which bends the whole tube sideways. It is best to drive opposite quadrants push-pull: a voltage on only one quadrant bends the tube in X or Y but also adds some unwanted net Z motion, while equal and opposite voltages on opposite quadrants bend the tube with no net Z motion. Orthogonal X-Y motion comes from driving the two quadrant pairs independently and adding the motions as vectors.

Z motion comes from applying the same voltage to all four outer quadrants. A positive voltage makes the whole tube contract; a negative voltage makes it extend. Important note: be careful with reverse-polarity drive. A strong field opposite to the poling direction will start to depolarize the ceramic, so keep reverse voltages well below the material's coercive field.

Tube Z movement (stretching mode) is calculated with:

ΔZ = d3,1 · V · L / h

where V is the voltage, L is the tube length, and h is the wall thickness. Note that the Z range depends only on the length and the wall thickness (thinner walls give more range), not on the tube diameter.

Tube X/Y movement (bending mode), with equal and opposite voltages on opposite quadrants, is calculated with:

ΔX = (2√2 / π) · d3,1 · Vx · L2 / (D · h)  ≈  0.90 · d3,1 · Vx · L2 / (D · h)

where Vx is the voltage applied to one quadrant (the opposite quadrant gets −Vx), and D is the mean diameter of the tube (outer diameter minus one wall thickness, i.e. measured at the middle of the wall). The same formula applies for Y. X/Y range grows with the square of the length and inversely with diameter and wall thickness, so long, narrow, thin-walled tubes scan the farthest.

The resonant frequencies of an unloaded tube are approximately:

fZ = (Y/ρ)½ / (4L)     fXY = 0.56 · (Y/ρ)½ · [D2 + (D − h)2]½ / (8L2)

where Y is the Young's modulus and ρ the density of the ceramic. Adding the mass of the tip assembly lowers both.

As an example, I specced out a tube from Boston Piezo-Optics: 0.25" (6.35 mm) outer diameter, 0.025" (0.635 mm) wall, 0.5" (12.7 mm) long, PZT-5H (d3,1 ≈ −265 pm/V), driven from a 250 V supply:

ΔZ / V = 265 pm/V · 12.7 mm / 0.635 mm ≈ 5.3 nm/V  →  ≈ 1.33 µm at 250 V

ΔX / V = 0.90 · 265 pm/V · (12.7 mm)2 / (5.715 mm · 0.635 mm) ≈ 10.6 nm/V  →  ≈ 2.65 µm at 250 V

(Using the outer diameter instead of the mean diameter, as in my original spreadsheet, gives about 9.5 nm/V and 2.39 µm. The difference is within the accuracy of the formula.) With the tip assembly mounted, the stretching resonance is around 33 kHz. The catch is the cost (about $115 for the tube) and the high-voltage drive electronics.

Piezo disk scanner (used in this STM)

Instead of a tube, my STM uses a cheap piezo buzzer disk as a "unimorph" scanner, following Dan Berard's and John Alexander's designs. It's far cheaper and runs on low voltage, but it isn't as stiff as a tube, so it has a much lower resonant frequency and is more sensitive to vibration.

A buzzer disk is a thin PZT disk bonded onto a larger brass disk. The brass is one electrode; the silvered top face of the PZT is the other. In my STM, the outer brass ring is the common electrode and is grounded. The PZT disk on top is cut into 4 separate quadrants with a craft knife, and a wire is attached to each quadrant. The tip standoff is glued to the center of the disk.

Above: The unimorph principle. The PZT layer (gray) is bonded to the brass (gold). When the PZT shrinks or grows in diameter and the brass doesn't, the disk dishes one way or the other.

Above: Quadrant layout. The four PZT quadrants (+X, −X, +Y, −Y) sit inside the outer brass ring, which is grounded.

Each quadrant gets a combination of the Z signal and either the X or the Y signal (the piezo driver on the main board does the summing):

V+X = Z + X    V−X = Z − X    V+Y = Z + Y    V−Y = Z − Y

  • Z (common mode): the same voltage on all four quadrants makes the PZT shrink (or grow) in diameter. Because the PZT is bonded to the brass, which doesn't change size, the disk dishes up or down like a bimetal strip, moving the center (and the tip) in Z.
  • X and Y (differential): opposite voltages on opposite quadrants make one side of the disk dish up and the other dish down. This tilts the center of the disk, and the tip at the end of its standoff swings sideways in X or Y.

Above: Differential drive tilts the disk and swings the tip sideways (X/Y); common-mode drive dishes the disk and moves the tip up and down (Z).

The right way to get the disk's real sensitivity is to calibrate it on a known sample: the HOPG lattice (0.246 nm atomic spacing) for X and Y, and HOPG step edges (0.335 nm per layer) for Z.

Scan pattern

The Y signal slowly ramps from min to max, either smoothly or stepping up one increment at the end of each scan line, while X follows a triangle wave. In practice this means two equal and opposite ramps on opposite quadrants for each axis. With the smooth scheme, there is slow Y movement during each X line, so the actual scan path is a compact zig-zag back and forth across the sample rather than a perfectly rectangular raster. Alternatively, the Y position can be stepped in software at the end of each scan line to form a regular raster.

According to Chen, it would be better to use a sine wave for the X scanning waveform to avoid abrupt stops and starts of the scanner, at the cost of somewhat more complicated plotting.

Each image pixel stores the Z position at that point. The controller already knows Z digitally, since it's the output of the PI loop that drives the Z DAC. The ADC only has to digitize the tunneling current, and a 16-bit ADC is plenty for that.

Piezoelectric nonlinearities and scanning error correction

There are two major sources of error when scanning with a piezoceramic.

1. Nonlinearity and hysteresis. Piezo displacement isn't perfectly proportional to voltage: the sensitivity (nm per volt) increases at larger drive amplitudes, so a linear voltage ramp gives a slightly non-linear sweep. Separately, hysteresis means the return path (X−) of the scanner doesn't retrace the extension path (X+). Because of this, most commercial scanners record both the X− and X+ legs but store them as two separate images rather than combining them. A homebrew STM should be able to acquire data on either leg, since image quality may be better in one direction. It's probably sufficient to choose before the scan which direction to use.

2. Creep. After a voltage step, the piezo moves most of the way quickly, then keeps drifting another few percent in the same direction over the next few minutes (roughly logarithmically in time). This distorts the start of a scan or anything right after a big move. The usual fixes are to wait after large moves (or throw away the first few lines), keep the offsets and scan size constant during an image, correct for it in software with a creep model, or drive the piezo with charge instead of voltage, which also reduces hysteresis.

You can determine the X and Y position either by measuring the actual X and Y driver voltages or by recording the commanded voltages (normally the latter).

Because of these errors, an uncorrected scan gives a somewhat distorted image. An error-correcting scheme for the driver voltages can be used for more accurate positioning and straighter scans. This normally takes the form of a look-up table (LUT) of modified drive voltages, with a different LUT for each scan size and speed.

2.3Current Measurement (Transimpedance Amplifier)

The most common approach to amplifying the tunneling current is a transimpedance amplifier (TIA). This is a current-to-voltage converter, most commonly built around an op-amp. TIAs are used to amplify the current output of many different devices, including photodetectors, accelerometers, and a huge variety of other sensors.

The gain is set by the feedback resistor. With 100 MΩ, every nanoamp of tunneling current becomes 100 mV at the output, so the 1-10 nA range around the setpoint becomes 0.1-1 V, easy for an ADC to read. The op-amp's own input bias current adds directly to the tunneling current, so it has to be far smaller than the signal. An ordinary op-amp, with a bias current in the nanoamp range, would swamp a 1 nA signal; a femtoamp-class amplifier is needed.

At these current levels, leakage across the surface of the circuit board also matters. The usual fix is a guard ring: the op-amp's inverting input sits at virtual ground, so the input is surrounded by a trace held at that same potential (ground). With no voltage between the input and the guard, essentially no leakage current can flow into the input; leakage from other nets flows into the guard instead.

2.4Noise and Vibration Isolation

Noise is the main problem for homemade STMs. The tip has to hold its height to a fraction of an ångström, so anything that moves the tip relative to the sample shows up in the image: building vibration, people walking nearby, sound, and electrical interference picked up by the very sensitive current amplifier.

Mechanical vibration is handled by hanging the scan head on soft springs. The springs and the suspended mass form a mechanical low-pass filter: vibrations well above the suspension's resonant frequency (around 1 Hz) are strongly attenuated before they reach the head. Slower motion passes straight through, but it moves the whole head together. Since the head is very stiff (its own resonances are in the tens of kHz and up), that common motion doesn't change the gap between the tip and the sample. The springs filter out the high frequencies, and the stiff head ignores the low ones.

A lightly damped suspension rings at its resonance, so some damping is added. Eddy current damping does this without any contact: a conductive plate moving through a magnetic field induces currents in the plate that oppose the motion.

Sound is handled by an enclosure: stiff walls block it, and a foam lining absorbs what gets in.

Electrical interference gets into a high-impedance current amplifier mainly through electric fields: mains hum coupling capacitively into the tip and the amplifier input, and static charge from people moving nearby. A grounded conductive shield blocks these, as long as it's connected to ground at a single point. Thin aluminum does very little against 60 Hz magnetic fields (that would take a high-permeability material like mu-metal), so magnetic pickup is handled by keeping wiring loops small and twisting signal pairs.

2.5Data Acquisition

The preamp output travels a few feet to the ADC on the main board, so it's measured differentially: both the preamp output and the preamp's ground reference are carried to the board and subtracted there, which cancels noise picked up equally on both wires.

The feedback loop runs on the Teensy 3.6. The Teensy takes the log of the ADC reading before feeding it into the PI loop. Because the tunneling current depends exponentially on the tip-sample distance, log(I) is linear in height, which keeps the loop gain the same no matter how far the current is from the setpoint. The log is done with a lookup table of 215 + 1 = 32,769 entries. The table is indexed by the absolute value of the signed 16-bit ADC reading. Signed 16-bit values run from −32,768 to +32,767, so the absolute value only spans 0 to 32,768: half of the 216 codes, plus one for |−32,768|. The PI loop output then drives the Z piezo signal.

2.6Spectroscopy

As a stretch goal, it's possible to use the STM for spectroscopy. Instead of scanning over the surface at a fixed bias voltage, the tip is held at one position (with the feedback loop paused) and the bias voltage is swept while recording the current, giving an I-V curve. The derivative, dI/dV, is roughly proportional to the sample's local density of electronic states (LDOS) at an energy eV relative to the Fermi level. That reveals things like band gaps and surface states, and how the electronic structure changes from place to place on the surface. It isn't a direct way to identify elements, and in air the curves are affected by surface contamination, but it's a fun next step. This technique is known as scanning tunneling spectroscopy (STS), or I-V and dI/dV spectroscopy. More info on this to come.

3Design of the STM

The design of my STM was influenced by a few other homemade STM designs (which can all be seen in the links at the end). In particular, the vibration isolation methods are quite similar, as there isn't a whole lot to change there. My eddy current damping system and acoustic damping are fairly unique, however.

3.1Mechanical Design

The scan head sits on a weighted platform that hangs from four springs inside an aluminum-extrusion enclosure, as shown in the CAD model below. The sections that follow cover the scan head, the tip, the vibration isolation, the coarse approach, and thermal drift.

The CAD model of the enclosure — an aluminum-extrusion frame with a door — a close-up of the scan head on its weighted disk with the plate hanging from four springs, the suspended platform from the front, and the frame built and lined with acoustic foam.

3.1.1Scan Head

The scan head is made of two 2" × 2" machined aluminum blocks, with drilled sockets for the fine-thread coarse approach screws and the piezo scanner. The three fine-thread (80 threads/inch) screws run through bushings in the top block. Their tips rest in a cone, a V-groove, and on a flat in the bottom block, following Dan Berard's design. This is a Kelvin kinematic coupling: the cone removes three degrees of freedom, the groove two, and the flat one, for exactly the six needed. The top block is located exactly, with no over-constraint, which makes the head rigid and repeatable. The screws are used for the coarse approach to the sample surface (see the Coarse Approach section below).

The piezo is a buzzer disk rated at 6.3 kHz, meant for sound, cut here into 4 separate quadrants. Applying the right combinations of voltages to the 4 quadrants scans the tip over the surface in X, Y and Z (see the Piezo Disk Scanner section above). The tip standoff is glued to the center of the disk through a glass spacer, which electrically isolates the tip and its tunneling current wire from the piezo drive electrodes. The tungsten tip is held in the standoff with a pin socket. The sample sits on a disk (a penny) on the bottom block, with the bias voltage applied to it by the electronics.

The small board mounted on the scan head is the preamplifier (see 3.2.1). The one pictured here is an early version from OSHPark.

3.1.2Scanning Electrode Tip

The tungsten tips are made by electrochemical etching, using the lamella drop-off technique. What matters for imaging is that the very end of the tip has a single atom, or a small cluster, that sticks out further than its neighbors. Because the tunneling current falls by about 10× per ångström, that frontmost atom carries almost all of the current. A cleanly etched tip has a sharp, reproducible apex with a much better chance of ending in a single atom, and it's mechanically stiffer than a ragged cut tip.

In the lamella technique, the etching only happens where the wire passes through a thin film (lamella) of electrolyte held in a wire loop, which you can see in the images below. The wire necks down inside the film for two reasons: the hydroxide concentration is uneven across the thin meniscus, and the soluble tungstate produced by the etch flows down the wire, forming a dense, viscous layer that protects the lower part. Eventually the neck gets thin enough that the lower part of the wire drops off, leaving a sharp point. There are more papers on how exactly this works in the Additional Links and Resources section at the bottom.

The best method I found in the literature uses 2 M NaOH (roughly 8 grams of NaOH dissolved in 100 mL of water) at around 4-4.5 V DC. The tungsten wire is the anode (+): it's the electrode being oxidized, dissolving as soluble tungstate. A thin steel wire bent into a loop is the cathode (−), where hydrogen bubbles form. A short section (about 1 cm) of 30 AWG tungsten wire was cut off with sharp wire cutters and placed through the steel loop.

The loop was dipped into the 2 M NaOH and lifted out to form a thin lamella of electrolyte across it, which started the etch. At first, I had trouble keeping the lamella intact, because hydrogen bubbles from the cathode kept breaking it. Adding a little acetone helped by keeping the bubbles from growing large enough to disrupt the film. In hindsight, acetone isn't a great choice: it isn't stable in strong base (it slowly condenses and turns the solution yellow). A drop of surfactant, like dish soap, does the same job more cleanly. The whole process took around 30 minutes, after which the etched tungsten wire dropped into a cup of water below and was recovered, being very careful not to touch the end and damage the tip.

Above: Tungsten wire inserted through NaOH lamella

3.1.3Noise Reduction and Vibration Isolation

How each of the measures from section 2.4 is implemented:

  • Spring suspension: the scan head sits on a steel-weighted platform hanging from four long extension springs (calculations below). The steel adds mass, which lowers the suspension's resonant frequencies.
  • Viton O-rings under the scan head and between the metal disks add damping.
  • Eddy current damping: a copper plate on the platform hangs just above a set of neodymium magnets, without touching them.
  • Enclosure: ¼" acrylic walls (STC 29) lined with 1.5" sound-absorbing foam (NRC 0.8).
  • Shielding: grounded aluminum foil sandwiched between the foam and the acrylic.
  • Wiring: very fine 40 AWG wire between the scan head and the electronics, so the cables don't carry vibration in.

Spring suspension calculations

The suspended platform (scan head, weights, and copper plate) weighs about 7.5 lb (3.40 kg) and hangs from four extension springs, each with a rate of 0.18 lb/in (31.5 N/m) and a free length of 11".

ktotal = 4 × 0.18 lb/in = 0.72 lb/in ≈ 126 N/m

Extension = 7.5 lb / 0.72 lb/in ≈ 10.4"  →  loaded length ≈ 11" + 10.4" ≈ 21.4"

The vertical (bouncing) resonance of a mass on a spring is:

fn = (1 / 2π) · √k / m = (1 / 2π) · √126 N/m / 3.40 kg ≈ 0.97 Hz

(My original spreadsheet gave 0.48 Hz because it used the rate of a single spring instead of all four.) The horizontal (swinging) resonance of the platform as a pendulum, with L ≈ 21.4" ≈ 0.544 m, is:

fp = (1 / 2π) · √g / L = (1 / 2π) · √9.81 m/s² / 0.544 m ≈ 0.68 Hz

How well the suspension isolates the head is described by the transmissibility T, the ratio of the vibration amplitude transmitted to the platform to the amplitude at the support:

T = √1 + (2ζr)2 / √(1 − r2)2 + (2ζr)2

where r = f / fn is the ratio of the vibration frequency to the resonant frequency, and ζ is the damping ratio (about 0.01 for a steel spring on its own). The main source of building vibration is HVAC equipment such as AC compressors, which the literature says is predominantly at 60 and 120 Hz:

Frequencyr = f / fnT (ζ = 0.01)Isolation
60 Hz620.0004299.96%
120 Hz1240.0001799.98%

The eddy current damping raises ζ, which trades away a little high-frequency isolation (even at ζ = 0.1, T at 60 Hz is still only about 0.003, or 99.7% isolation) in exchange for quickly killing the slow bouncing and swinging of the suspension near 1 Hz.

3.1.4Coarse Approach

The coarse approach is currently manual, with plans to add a stepper motor later.

The top block of the scan head sits on the three 80 threads/inch screws, so one full turn of a screw moves it 1 / 80" = 317.5 µm. Two of the screws form a pivot line, and the third acts as a lever. The tip sits much closer to the pivot line than the third screw does, so turning the third screw moves the tip only about 1/30 as much (about a 30:1 mechanical advantage). One full turn of that screw moves the tip about 317.5 µm / 30 ≈ 10.6 µm, and a small hand twist (about 1/50 of a turn) moves it about 200 nm. That's comfortably smaller than the piezo's ~700 nm Z range, so the tip can be walked in by hand without crashing. With the Z feedback loop enabled (which fully extends the piezo while there is no current), I advance the lever screw in small twists until the tunneling indicator LED comes on.

Planned: a 28BYJ-48 geared stepper motor (about 2048 steps per revolution) on the lever screw would give steps of roughly 317.5 µm / 2048 / 30 ≈ 5 nm at the tip. That would allow an automated "woodpecker" approach: fully extend the Z piezo while watching for tunneling current; if there's none, retract, step the motor once, and repeat. Since each motor step is far smaller than the piezo's Z range, the tip finds the surface before it can crash into it. The motor's gearbox has some backlash, so the steps won't be perfectly uniform, but that doesn't matter for this approach.

3.1.5Thermal Drift

Temperature changes make every part of the scan head expand or contract, which slowly changes the tip-to-sample gap. To estimate how much, I followed the path from the sample surface up through the support structure and back down through the tip assembly:

Above: Simplified cross-section of the scan head used for the thermal drift estimate.

  • Going up from the sample: the steel screws carry the top block. The part of each screw threaded into the 12.7 mm aluminum top block is locked to the aluminum by the threads, so it expands with the aluminum. The 12.3 mm of steel below the block expands as steel.
  • Coming back down to the tip: the brass disk rests on top of the top block, and the 3 mm glass spacer, the 20 mm stainless steel standoff, and the 2 mm of exposed tungsten tip hang down from it.
  • The bottom block doesn't matter: the sample and the screw tips both sit on its top surface, so its expansion doesn't change the gap.

The change in gap per kelvin is the expansion of the support minus the expansion of the hanging tip assembly:

ΔGap / ΔT = (12.7 mm · αAl) + (12.3 mm · αsteel) − (3 mm · αglass) − (20 mm · αsteel) − (2 mm · αW)

= (12.7 · 23.6) + (12.3 · 17.3) − (3 · 5) − (20 · 17.3) − (2 · 4.5) nm/K ≈ 140 nm/K

using the thermal expansion coefficients (α, in 10−6/K) from the table below. The gap grows as the head warms up, because the support expands more than the tip assembly. The result is sensitive to the material values. My original slide used α = 14.4 for the steel, which gives about 165 nm/K. My original slide also subtracted the brass and PZT thicknesses to account for the disk (giving 136 nm/K), but the disk's real thermal effect is different: PZT on brass is a bimetal (7.5 vs 18.9 × 10−6/K), so the disk bows with temperature, which moves the tip in Z on top of the stack expansion. Overall, ~150 nm/K is the right order of magnitude.

The piezo disk's Z range is about 700 nm, so a temperature change of only about 700 / 140 ≈ 5 K is enough to drift the tip out of the piezo's range, at which point the coarse approach has to be adjusted. Smaller, slower drift is absorbed by the feedback loop and shows up as a slow tilt or offset in the images. In practice, this is only important for long scans (more than about 10 minutes); for short scans the room temperature barely changes. The same expansion also happens sideways, which shows up as lateral drift that distorts long scans.

The ideal fix is to build the head from a material whose expansion matches the piezo's. Many people use Macor (a machinable glass-ceramic) because its thermal expansion is close to PZT's. That matters most for tube scanners, but a Macor head would reduce drift here too.

The last column, (Y/ρ)½, is the speed of sound v in the material, which sets how stiff each part is for its size. The lowest longitudinal resonance of a part of length L is about:

f = v / 2L = √Y / (4L2ρ)

For the 25 mm aluminum structure, that's about 5200 / (2 × 0.025) ≈ 100 kHz, and similarly high for the steel parts. These are far above the building vibration frequencies and well above the piezo disk's ~3.4 kHz resonance, so the disk is the softest part of the head, and the rest can be treated as rigid.

3.2Electronics Design

The electrical design is split into a few sections. The first is the scanning side, which drives the piezo disk to scan over the surface and to move the tip toward it. This uses a 4-channel 16-bit DAC8814, with sigma-delta modulation to get about 20 bits of effective resolution. The other side applies the bias voltage to the sample and measures the tunneling current: it amplifies the current, reads it with a 16-bit ADC, and sends the data over USB to the computer. It also closes the feedback loop for the scanner: if the current is too high, move the tip up a bit. The tunneling current falls off exponentially with distance (about 10× per ångström), so tiny changes in height produce large, easily measured changes in current.

Above: Overall electronic design, courtesy of Dan Berard.

3.2.1Pre-amplifier

The preamp is a transimpedance amplifier (see 2.3) on its own small board, mounted at the scan head so the wire from the tip is as short as possible. It uses the ADA4530-1 op-amp, whose input bias current is guaranteed below 20 fA at room temperature, with a 100 MΩ feedback resistor and a guard ring around the input. I designed it in Eagle, following MatthiasR's design on Hackaday and Dan Berard's design, and it has gone through a few revisions. The setpoint is around 1 nA, but there's headroom up to about 100 nA so the preamp doesn't saturate when the current spikes.

References: Dan Berard's STM electronics and MatthiasR's DIY STM on Hackaday.

Above: Versions 1 and 2 of the preamplifier. Both were sent to OSHPark and should be done within a week or so.

3.2.2Main Analog Scanning PCB

The main board is a 4-layer PCB with separate ground and power planes to keep noise down, since every signal on it needs to be precise. It carries:

  • The DAC8814, a 4-channel 16-bit DAC that generates the X, Y and Z scan signals and the sample bias, sigma-delta dithered by the firmware to about 20 bits.
  • The piezo driver, which sums Z with X and Y to make the four quadrant voltages (Z ± X, Z ± Y).
  • LT1469 op-amps that buffer and filter the preamp output and its ground reference.
  • The LTC2326-16, a 16-bit pseudo-differential ADC that digitizes the tunneling current.

It runs from ±15 V, +5 V and +3.3 V supplies, connects to the scan head through DB9 connectors, and plugs into the Teensy 3.6.

Above: Some of the electrical schematics and beginning of the board design files.

3.3Software Design

The programming is split into two parts, and will probably be where I spend most of my time on this project. The first is the firmware on the Teensy 3.6, written in C/C++, which controls all the electronics in the instrument and sends the data to the computer over USB. The second is the host software in C#, which receives the data and builds the image from it, line by line.

3.3.1Teensy 3.6 Firmware

The Teensy uses a timer to call a function at a regular interval (every 40 µs). This function increments the scan, performs the PI calculations to update the Z axis, and performs sigma-delta modulation of the scanner DACs. Since the tip is a sharp, fragile solid object, its velocity needs to be controlled at all times; if it moves too fast, it will likely crash into something. So the tip scans in a zig-zag pattern rather than a traditional raster (as in a CRT or an SEM, which jumps back to the start of each line). Since the scan always increments at the same time interval, a slower scan acquires more data. All samples acquired within a pixel are averaged, so a slower scan has less noise. Once a line of the image has been scanned in one direction and re-scanned in the other, the Teensy sends the Z data and error signal data over USB.

Many thanks to the other STM builders out there for their help and guidance on the software end. In particular, Dan Berard's page and John Alexander's page were very helpful with the software!

Above: Teensy software in the Arduino environment.

3.3.2C# Host Software

The PC software receives the data, does some basic processing, and adds it to the images on the screen. The left image is the topography (Z signal) and the right image is the error signal. The error signal looks like the gradient of the Z signal and has more high-frequency content. The topography data is displayed line by line in the graph at the bottom of the window. The red curve is the most recent scan line, and the blue curve is the same line scanned in the opposite direction. Ideally, these curves would be identical, but they will never match perfectly because of piezo hysteresis and similar effects.

5Results

I scanned two samples: HOPG (highly oriented pyrolytic graphite) and electroplated gold. HOPG is easy to prepare: peeling off the top layers with tape leaves a fresh, atomically flat surface. On HOPG, the microscope resolved individual carbon atoms (the image at the top of this page is the same scan shown in false color). Some drift is visible in the image, likely from thermal drift or vibration.

HOPG surface, raw grayscale data. The bright spots are carbon atoms.

Other metals are harder to image in air. Their surfaces oxidize quickly, which is one reason commercial STMs operate in vacuum, and their electrons are spread out across the surface, which gives much less atomic contrast than graphite.

Getting a good image means homing in on the scan parameters: line rate, scan size, the P and I gains, and resolution all interact. Each scan also gives a sense of the impact of thermal drift, noise, and piezo hysteresis.

Electroplated gold. The middle and right scans were taken while tuning the scan parameters.
